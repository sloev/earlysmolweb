# Iran — early internet activity, networks, and web research

**Status:** active scouting; no earliest-personal-website claim verified.  
**Focus:** individual-authored pages from the mid-1990s through the first Persian-language weblog wave; distinguish Iran-resident authors from Persian-language/diaspora authors.

## Early connectivity and hosting context (not personal-page evidence)

- Payman Arabshahi's contemporaneous survey, updated 9 June 1997, says Iran's early internet activity was led by the Institute for Studies in Theoretical Physics and Mathematics (IPM), with academic users served through university/research connections. It lists the two leading 1997 presidential candidates' websites and links to commercial and academic web directories. These are routes for further link extraction, not personal-page candidates by themselves.  
  Source: https://iranian.com/WebGuide/InternetIran/InternetIran.html
- An NSRC-hosted report dated 13 November 1995 lists early academic network hosts at IPM, Sharif University, University of Tehran, University of Guilan and other institutions, plus early private-sector email/FTP/news/mailing-list access through Neda Rayaneh. The host list may lead to personal directories, account pages, Gopher material, and staff/student home directories.  
  Source: https://www.nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=IR&id=890202487943:497427936
- A later NSRC survey says Neda Rayaneh began a BBS in early 1994 and internet access in February 1995. Treat this as secondary infrastructure context until cross-checked.  
  Source: https://nsrc.org/regions/MIDEAST/IR/internet-iran-2001.pdf

## Candidate lead 1: Salman Jariri's Persian weblog (2001)

- Person/project: Salman Jariri / Salman’s Weblog.
- Reported first post: 7 September 2001.
- Reported original URL: http://www.globalpersian.com/salman/weblog.html
- Medium: manually coded personal weblog; Persian language.
- Status: **candidate for early Persian-language personal publishing; not established as the earliest personal website associated with Iran.**
- A 2007 peer-reviewed article by Gholam Khiabany and Annabelle Sreberny identifies Jariri's weblog as the first Iranian weblog and gives the original URL in a footnote. A 2018 University of Minnesota dissertation dates the first post to 7 September 2001 and describes its introductory definition of a blog as a personal writing homepage.
- Attribution caveat: Persian-language or Iranian identity does not prove that the page was created while its author was resident in Iran. A 2010 Iranian.com profile says Jariri was then living in Dubai; his location in September 2001 still needs confirmation.

## Additional primary-source clarification — 16 November 2026

The contemporaneous 13 November 1995 NSRC report is now inspected in full: https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=IR&id=890202487943:497427936

It lists named institutional hostnames and workstation/server names across IPM, Sharif University, Tehran University, Guilan University, and other research institutions. These names are infrastructure leads, **not identified personal homepages**; the report does not expose personal user-directory URLs. It also reproduces Neda Rayaneh's 1995 announcement: Neda's BBS began in spring 1994; by early 1995 it offered Internet email and other offline network services. Individual accounts were available, but the 1995 report says the service was still limited to email and FTP/news/mailing lists via email, with no direct telnet/FTP access. This makes Neda a promising source for early personal online expression and email/BBS culture, but it does not demonstrate that subscribers could publish public web pages in 1995.

A later NSRC survey describes Neda's 1995–1996 hosting and ISP history, but says its web hosting was later discontinued on the public internet and retained on an intranet. This retrospective must not be used to infer an earlier personal homepage without a specific dated page or archive capture: https://nsrc.org/regions/MIDEAST/IR/internet-iran-2001.pdf

The 1995 host list does, however, provide concrete next extraction targets: physics.ipm.ac.ir and cc.sharif.ac.ir are explicitly described as Gopher servers; the old Sharif host list includes named machines such as maryam, nasrin, mina, laleh, and sina, but these are hostnames, not people. Search their historical Gopher menus, public_html conventions, and 1995–1997 university web directories before drawing conclusions.

## 1996 named-contact and Gopher leads

The July 1996 *Iran Internet Points of Contact: Academic Research and Teaching* directory is a more useful extraction source than the raw 1995 hostname list. It names individual technical contacts and provides institution-level web/Gopher endpoints. Examples include Zahra Hashemi (Institute for International Energy Studies), Parisa Amiri Sani (International Institute of Earthquake Engineering & Seismology), Armin Madadkar (National Research Center for Genetic Engineering & Biotechnology), Kamran Shokoufandeh (Guilan University), Shahriar Pourazin (Sharif University), and named contacts at Tehran University and other institutions. These are **named contact leads, not personal-page candidates** unless a separately authored personal URL is found.

The directory also identifies public-facing web/Gopher roots worth checking for personal subdirectories and linked pages: `gopher://physics.ipm.ac.ir`, `gopher://cc.sharif.ac.ir`, `gopher://kadous.gu.ac.ir`, `gopher://toos.um.ac.ir`, and `gopher://genetic.nrcgeb.ac.ir`; web roots include `khorshid.ut.ac.ir`, `linux.ce.sharif.ac.ir`, and `pearl.sums.ac.ir`. It notes that the Sharif campus network had hundreds of machines and at least 100 internet-connected, making its Gopher menus and computing-centre directories especially promising extraction targets. Source: https://iranian.com/July96/Features/InternetIran/InternetIran-Academic.html (last updated 13 July 1996).

The companion July 1996 survey reports over 1,100 usernames on IPM's main server as of April 1996, although many were shared institutional accounts; it also reports at least 500 Iranian domains with valid IP addresses by January 1996. These figures suggest a broad pool of possible personal expression, but do not establish public personal pages. Source: https://iranian.com/July96/Features/InternetIran/InternetIran.html

## Research notes

- “First Iranian blog” is ambiguous: first Persian-language weblog, first weblog by an Iranian author, first weblog by someone resident in Iran, or first personal web publication in Iran. These are not interchangeable.
- The earliest Persian-language blog is not necessarily the earliest personal homepage. Continue searching pre-2001 personal homepages, personal directories, student/staff pages, Geocities/Tripod/Angelfire pages, Gopher personal pages, and BBS-to-web transitions.
- The 1995 NSRC host list and 1997 Payman Arabshahi survey are promising link-extraction sources. Examine named university domains and old hostnames for personal user directories, student pages, and Gopher content.
- Search Persian and Finglish spellings, transliterations, and English-language diaspora sources. Prioritize women, artists, students, regional communities, and pages outside the Tehran institutional network; the surviving English-language record may overrepresent elite technical institutions and diaspora publishers.

## Broadened chronology — academic networking, email, and TCP/IP (16 November 2026)

The broadened scope surfaces an important distinction in Iran's chronology: an early academic email connection through EARN/BITNET preceded the country's documented TCP/IP Internet connection.

### Early network milestones

- **1987 — reported origins of the effort.** The 1999 *Iran’s Telecom and Internet Sector: A Comprehensive Survey* says the effort to bring the Internet to Iran dates to 1987. It does not identify a specific operational node or user service in that year, so this is a historical background lead, not a verified connectivity milestone. Source: https://nsrc.org/regions/MIDEAST/IR/internet-iran-2001.pdf, §2.1 (report dated 15 June 1999; grade B/C).
- **January 1993 — IPM's EARN/BITNET research link and first reported email.** The same survey describes a 9600-baud leased line from the Institute for Studies in Theoretical Physics and Mathematics (IPM), Tehran, to the University of Vienna, initially through Iran's membership in EARN/BITNET. It identifies the first email from Iran as a greeting sent by IPM director Mohammad Javad Larijani to Vienna University administrators. This is a strong milestone for academic networked communication and international email; do not label it an established national TCP/IP connection. Source: same NSRC survey, §2.1 (grade B).
- **Mid-1994 — transition to TCP/IP Internet access.** The survey's IPM institutional profile says that in mid-1994 IPM modified its BITNET/TERENA gateway to support TCP/IP, establishing Internet access for IREARN members. It records 512 assigned IP addresses and says at least 18 government universities and research centres were connected through IPM; access remained concentrated among some faculty and graduate students because campus LANs were scarce. This is the clearest dated source located so far for operational TCP/IP access in Iran. Source: same NSRC survey, §4.3.12, pp. 55–56 (grade B).
- **1996 — contemporary description, but date wording is imprecise.** Payman Arabshahi's survey, last updated 1 October 1996, says Iran's entrance into the global data network was spearheaded by IPM “some four and a half years ago,” initially via BITNET/EARN, later becoming a full Internet link with 500 IP addresses. This could imply an earlier 1992 start, but the wording does not separate email transport from TCP/IP and conflicts with the more precise January 1993 and mid-1994 chronology in the later technical survey. Preserve the discrepancy rather than choosing a single “arrival” date. Source: https://iranian.com/Sep96/Web/InternetIran/InternetIran.html (contemporary survey; grade B).
- **1995–1996 — broader service ecology.** The 1996 survey describes tens of thousands of users, academic networks and expanding bulletin boards; the 1999 technical survey inventories numerous commercial ISPs and institutional networks. These are useful for tracing the transition from research access to public and commercial service, but they are not evidence of an earlier personal homepage by themselves. Sources: 1996 survey above; 1999 NSRC technical survey, especially the ISP inventory (grade B).
