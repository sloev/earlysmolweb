## Additional primary-source clarification — 16 November 2026

The contemporaneous 13 November 1995 NSRC report is now inspected in full: https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=IR&id=890202487943:497427936

It lists named institutional hostnames and workstation/server names across IPM, Sharif University, Tehran University, Guilan University, and other research institutions. These names are infrastructure leads, **not identified personal homepages**; the report does not expose personal user-directory URLs. It also reproduces Neda Rayaneh's 1995 announcement: Neda's BBS began in spring 1994; by early 1995 it offered Internet email and other offline network services. Individual accounts were available, but the 1995 report says the service was still limited to email and FTP/news/mailing lists via email, with no direct telnet/FTP access. This makes Neda a promising source for early personal online expression and email/BBS culture, but it does not demonstrate that subscribers could publish public web pages in 1995.

A later NSRC survey describes Neda's 1995–1996 hosting and ISP history, but says its web hosting was later discontinued on the public internet and retained on an intranet. This retrospective must not be used to infer an earlier personal homepage without a specific dated page or archive capture: https://nsrc.org/regions/MIDEAST/IR/internet-iran-2001.pdf

The 1995 host list does, however, provide concrete next extraction targets: physics.ipm.ac.ir and cc.sharif.ac.ir are explicitly described as Gopher servers; the old Sharif host list includes named machines such as maryam, nasrin, mina, laleh, and sina, but these are hostnames, not people. Search their historical Gopher menus, public_html conventions, and 1995–1997 university web directories before drawing conclusions.

## Broadened chronology — academic networking, email, and TCP/IP (16 November 2026)

The broadened scope surfaces an important distinction in Iran's chronology: an early academic email connection through EARN/BITNET preceded the country's documented TCP/IP Internet connection.

### Early network milestones

- **1987 — reported origins of the effort.** The 1999 *Iran’s Telecom and Internet Sector: A Comprehensive Survey* says the effort to bring the Internet to Iran dates to 1987. It does not identify a specific operational node or user service in that year, so this is a historical background lead, not a verified connectivity milestone. Source: https://nsrc.org/regions/MIDEAST/IR/internet-iran-2001.pdf, §2.1 (report dated 15 June 1999; grade B/C).
- **January 1993 — IPM's EARN/BITNET research link and first reported email.** The same survey describes a 9600-baud leased line from the Institute for Studies in Theoretical Physics and Mathematics (IPM), Tehran, to the University of Vienna, initially through Iran's membership in EARN/BITNET. It identifies the first email from Iran as a greeting sent by IPM director Mohammad Javad Larijani to Vienna University administrators. This is a strong milestone for academic networked communication and international email; do not label it an established national TCP/IP connection. Source: same NSRC survey, §2.1 (grade B).
- **Mid-1994 — transition to TCP/IP Internet access.** The survey's IPM institutional profile says that in mid-1994 IPM modified its BITNET/TERENA gateway to support TCP/IP, establishing Internet access for IREARN members. It records 512 assigned IP addresses and says at least 18 government universities and research centres were connected through IPM; access remained concentrated among some faculty and graduate students because campus LANs were scarce. This is the clearest dated source located so far for operational TCP/IP access in Iran. Source: same NSRC survey, §4.3.12, pp. 55–56 (grade B).
- **1996 — contemporary description, but date wording is imprecise.** Payman Arabshahi's survey, last updated 1 October 1996, says Iran's entrance into the global data network was spearheaded by IPM “some four and a half years ago,” initially via BITNET/EARN, later becoming a full Internet link with 500 IP addresses. This could imply an earlier 1992 start, but the wording does not separate email transport from TCP/IP and conflicts with the more precise January 1993 and mid-1994 chronology in the later technical survey. Preserve the discrepancy rather than choosing a single “arrival” date. Source: https://iranian.com/Sep96/Web/InternetIran/InternetIran.html (contemporary survey; grade B).
- **1995–1996 — broader service ecology.** The 1996 survey describes tens of thousands of users, academic networks and expanding bulletin boards; the 1999 technical survey inventories numerous commercial ISPs and institutional networks. These are useful for tracing the transition from research access to public and commercial service, but they are not evidence of an earlier personal homepage by themselves. Sources: 1996 survey above; 1999 NSRC technical survey, especially the ISP inventory (grade B).

## Broadened chronology — EARN/BITNET and TCP/IP milestones (16 November 2026)

### January 1993 — IREARN EARN/BITNET link

A contemporaneous NSRC Connectivity Provider Database entry, updated 29 October 1993 under the title “OCLB IR - EARN/Bitnet Link to Iran,” records the IPM Computer Division contact Ali Shokofandeh and says the BITNET link had been “up and reliable since January 1993.” It also names Ebrahim N. Mashayekh and Akbar Behzadi as contacts at IREARN.BITNET. This is direct contemporary evidence for Iran's academic EARN/BITNET connectivity and associated networked email, not by itself evidence of a native TCP/IP Internet connection.

- Source: https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=IR&id=890202488551:497423506
- Evidence grade: **A/B** (contemporaneous provider-database record based on direct contact; the January start date is reported in October 1993).
- Category: academic network / BITNET-EARN / email; operator: academic; network status: inter-network / academic network, distinct from the later TCP/IP Internet milestone.

### Mid-1994 — IPM gateway converted to TCP/IP

The 15 June 1999 *Iran's Telecom and Internet Sector: A Comprehensive Survey* states that IPM modified its BITNET gateway to TERENA/EARN in mid-1994 to support TCP/IP, establishing an Internet connection for IREARN members. It records 512 assigned IP addresses, the existing 9.6 kbps Vienna link, and access for at least 18 government universities and research centres. It further notes that use was initially concentrated among some faculty and graduate students because most universities lacked LANs.

- Source: https://nsrc.org/regions/MIDEAST/IR/internet-iran-2001.pdf (section 4.3.12, pp. 55–56 in the report; lines 2054–2060 in the extracted text).
- Evidence grade: **B** (detailed near-contemporary technical survey; retrospective to 1994).
- Category: direct TCP/IP Internet connectivity / academic gateway; operator: academic; network status: Internet.

### Interpretation and remaining distinction

The two milestones should not be collapsed: the October 1993 NSRC record documents a reliable EARN/BITNET link since January 1993, while the 1999 survey dates IPM's TCP/IP conversion to mid-1994. A 1996 retrospective survey's “four and a half years” phrasing remains imprecise and is not sufficient to move the TCP/IP date earlier. Continue seeking original IPM/IREARN records, surviving mailing-list archives, Usenet/news access records, Gopher menus and FTP archives. These network milestones do not establish a personal homepage or public web service.

## Provider-level chronology — Neda and Virayeshgar (1994–1995)

### Spring 1994 — Neda's Persian BBS opens; autumn 1994 Internet email

The 13 November 1995 NSRC report reproduces a Neda announcement dated 2 March 1995 by Mani Shahrir. It describes Neda as Iran's first Persian BBS, started in spring 1994, with Persian email, chat, file transfer and databases available from its first release. The announcement says Neda's first Internet email service began in autumn 1994 over a PPP connection; initially messages were queued and transferred at set times by an operator. A later improvement, described as using SLIP over a leased line via IPM, made mail transfer continuous rather than operator-mediated. The announcement calls the BBS itself free and distinguishes it from the separately charged Internet email service.

- Contemporary source: https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=IR&id=890202487943:497427936 (13 Nov 1995 archive entry; reproduced Neda announcement dated 2 Mar 1995).
- Evidence grade: **A/B** for the dated 1995 announcement and its retrospective service chronology.
- Categories: Persian-language BBS/community; Internet email gateway; chat and file/database services. Do not call the BBS itself an Internet service merely because it later offered Internet email.

The same 1995 NSRC report includes an IPM announcement dated 14 February 1995 saying Neda offered mail and offline network services over two leased lines to IPM, with 16 initial dial-up lines and the option to expand. This corroborates Neda's provider role and the early distinction between online BBS access and Internet services delivered through email.

### June 1994 — Virayeshgar BBS, UUCP email and Usenet echoes

The 15 June 1999 *Iran's Telecom and Internet Sector: A Comprehensive Survey* describes Virayeshgar (Vira), a Tehran-based software company, as operating a local BBS with Internet email service from **June 1994**. The BBS used two 33.6 kbps modems and UUCP twice daily to exchange email through HOLONET in the San Francisco Bay Area. Users received addresses in the firstname.lastname@vira.com form. The service also offered offline FTP/Archie/WWW/news by email and echoed some technical Usenet newsgroups. The report explicitly lists “No web presence – email services only.”

- Source: https://nsrc.org/regions/MIDEAST/IR/internet-iran-2001.pdf (section 4.3.30, pp. 78–79; extracted lines 2950–2977).
- Evidence grade: **B** (detailed 1999 provider survey, retrospective to 1994).
- Categories: UUCP email gateway; offline Internet information retrieval; Usenet newsgroup echo; local BBS. Network status: inter-network via UUCP; not evidence of a public website or a direct TCP/IP link at launch.
- Attribution: provider based in Tehran; the report also lists a US mailing address, so retain the local operating base and overseas relay distinction.

### April 1994 snapshot — domain registration did not equal working Internet access

A contemporaneous NSRC response dated 18 April 1994 says Iran's domain had been registered and four IPM hosts appeared in DNS, but external observers could not reach the connections that morning. It lists the IPM network contacts and name servers. This is a useful negative checkpoint: registered domain names and DNS host records preceded, or at least did not guarantee, usable end-to-end connectivity at that date.

- Source: https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=IR&id=890202488412:497424948
- Evidence grade: **A/B** (contemporary operational observation, though a single-day check cannot prove that no connectivity existed anywhere in Iran).

### Chronology interpretation

Keep the provider-specific routes distinct:
- Spring 1994: Neda's Persian BBS begins (local dial-up service).
- June 1994: Vira's BBS begins UUCP-based Internet email, offline FTP/Archie/WWW/news delivery and some Usenet echoes (1999 retrospective).
- Autumn 1994: Neda begins Internet email over PPP, initially in scheduled batches (1995 contemporary announcement).
- February 1995: Neda's improved SLIP/leased-line email service is described as continuously online; the NSRC's 2001 survey dates Neda's broader Internet access to February 1995.
- Mid-1994: IPM's EARN gateway is converted to TCP/IP for IREARN members (1999 survey); the 18 April 1994 NSRC observation cautions against assuming that DNS registration meant a working public connection earlier in that year.

These are distinct BBS, UUCP, email-gateway and TCP/IP milestones. None independently establishes an early personal homepage. The Vira record does, however, open a concrete route into Iranian Usenet history: identify which technical groups were echoed and search surviving posts/archives for Iranian authors and node headers.

## Newly surfaced evidence — first EARN access and the IRNET/IRANET naming problem (16 November 2026)

### Late 1992 — first EARN access, before the January 1993 operational checkpoint

A 2009 Persian-language interview with **Siavash Shahshahani**, IPM's deputy director and a participant in the project, adds a first-person chronology. Shahshahani says Iran first connected to the University of Linz by dial-up telephone in **late 1992**, then moved to a leased line to the University of Vienna, an EARN hub. The contemporary NSRC node record independently lists IREARN's EARN connection date as **1 December 1992**, with membership dated 1 November and a record update of 9 December; the January 1993 NSRC report then documents the registered node and a successful reachability check. The combined evidence supports a cautious sequence: initial EARN access by late 1992 (retrospective participant testimony), registered node connection dated 1 December 1992 (contemporary registry metadata), and independently confirmed EARN/BITNET reachability by 19 January 1993.

The same interview says IP/ICP was enabled in **late 1993**, which would place TCP/IP earlier than the mid-1994 date in the 1999 NSRC technical survey. It also says early addresses ended in `.univie.at` before Iran obtained `.ir` in April 1995. This is an important first-person conflict, but the interview is retrospective and does not give a dated router record or packet trace. Retain **mid-1994** as the best-specified TCP/IP milestone for now, while explicitly recording the late-1993 recollection as an unresolved alternative; do not silently reconcile them.

- Shahshahani interview (Persian, published 2 May 2009): https://news.gooya.com/technology/archives/2009/05/087038print.php (questions on initial connection and IP/ICP at lines 9–16).
- Contemporary NSRC IREARN node record (19 Jan 1993; embedded connect date 1 Dec 1992): https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=IR&id=890202488663:497420738.
- Contemporary NSRC BITNET/EARN reliability report (29 Oct 1993): https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=IR&id=890202488551:497423506.
- Retrospective technical survey (15 Jun 1999; mid-1994 TCP/IP gateway conversion): https://nsrc.org/regions/MIDEAST/IR/internet-iran-2001.pdf (section 4.3.12).
- Evidence grade: **A/B** for the 1992 registration date and January 1993 reachability; **C** for the late-1992 Linz dial-up and late-1993 IP/ICP recollection.

### 1993 founding claim — Pilot Iran's IRNET / “Information and Communication Network of Iran”

Payman Arabshahi's survey, updated **9 June 1997**, has a provider entry titled **IRNET**. It says the service was founded in 1993 by N. J. Rad as a subsidiary of the private Iranian company Pilot Iran; describes a large dial-up BBS (telephone number recorded in the survey) that was then porting its content onto the Web; and lists Internet access, email, server hosting, website design (WWW/FTP/Gopher), electronic publishing and commercial online services. It gives a Tehran address, the domain `irnet.net.ir`, and describes the then-current international link as DCI's 64-kbit/s route via Gulfsat/Kuwait. This is a substantial lead for an early commercial BBS-to-Internet provider and online-publishing service, but the source's present-tense wording does **not** prove that full Internet access or Web publishing was already operational in 1993. Use 1993 only as the reported founding date; the survey is a 1997 snapshot of the later service.

There is a naming ambiguity that must be preserved. The same 1997 survey separately calls the **national academic network** “IRANET” (associated with IPM), while its commercial provider entry is spelled **IRNET** and expands the name as “Information and Communication Network of Iran.” A 2003 article instead spells the Pilot Iran commercial service **IRANET** and describes it as a large BBS with Internet access, email, electronic publishing and website design. Until a contemporary corporate or domain record resolves the spelling and identity, refer to this as **Pilot Iran's IRNET (also rendered IRANET in later secondary accounts)** and do not conflate it with the academic network label.

- Payman Arabshahi, *The Internet in Iran: A Survey*, updated 9 June 1997: https://www.iranian.com/WebGuide/InternetIran/InternetIran.html (IRNET provider entry, lines 242–265; academic IRANET label at lines 72–76).
- Babak Rahimi, “Cyberdissent: The Internet in Revolutionary Iran,” *MERIA* 7.3 (September 2003): https://ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu/olj/meria/meria03_rab01.pdf (pp. 101–102 / PDF lines 28–33; calls the private service IRANET and dates its creation to 1993).
- Evidence grade: **B/C** for the reported 1993 founding and later service profile (near-contemporary 1997 survey plus 2003 scholarly retrospective); exact 1993 launch day, early BBS operation logs, and first Web-hosting date remain unknown.
- Category: commercial/community BBS; ISP and access service; email; web/FTP/Gopher design and hosting; electronic publishing. Country connection: operator and service address in Tehran, Iran.
- Next: recover `irnet.net.ir` historical DNS/WHOIS and Wayback captures; search contemporary Iranian trade press and 1994–1996 network directories for N. J. Rad, Pilot Iran and the BBS number; determine whether the 1993 system initially operated as a local BBS, an Internet-connected BBS, or both, and date the first public web service separately.

## New diaspora and collective-web leads — 18 November 2026

### IHRWG — an Iran-focused human-rights website documented by October 1997

The Iranian Human Rights Working Group (IHRWG) is a strong new **collective-site** lead. A contemporary IHRWG announcement reproduced by The Iranian on 13 October 1997 directs readers to sign an online petition at www.ihrwg.org/CP/ and describes the group's web campaign. The embedded signature list is marked as current on 24 October 1997, so the surviving copy appears to have been updated after its header date; record only **October 1997** as the earliest located evidence of the site, not a launch date. A 4 September 1998 IHRWG statement again directs readers to its web pages at www.ihrwg.org, and a Canadian Immigration and Refugee Board response published on 23 July 1998 says the site was accessed on 25 June 1998. These are independent contemporary references to a functioning site.

A later IHRWG history says the group formed in March 1994 through discussions on soc.culture.iranian; 14 participants became founding members, adopted a charter and elected a steering committee in May 1994, after which the group officially launched. This dates the **online community**, not the website. The group was transnational and its 1998 contact details included a New Jersey postal address and a Stanford-hosted email address; no evidence found here establishes that the site was hosted in Iran or that its operators were resident there. The original ihrwg.org pages are no longer active, and no Wayback capture was recovered in this pass.

### Sam Ghandchi — directly archived soc.culture.iranian post, 14 June 1994
