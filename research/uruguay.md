# Uruguay — early internet activity, networks, and web research

**Status:** active scouting; no earliest-personal-page candidate verified.  
**Focus:** locate personal pages made possible by early academic connectivity and public web hosting in the mid-1990s; search Spanish-language sources and archives.

## Working leads

### 1. Chasque public web hosting from August 1995

A study of community networks in Buenos Aires and Montevideo reports that Chasque, a Uruguayan NGO/community network, began providing Internet access to the general public in August 1995. It says Uruguayans could then browse the web and upload their own pages without registration or institutional affiliation, with charges billed to telephone accounts. This is an unusually promising lead for personal expression: identify Chasque hosting conventions, old user pages, and surviving directories/captures. The study is secondary; seek contemporary Chasque documentation and archive evidence.

Source: https://www.academia.edu/887878/Community_networks_go_virtual_Tracing_the_evolution_of_ICT_in_Buenos_Aires_and_Montevideo

### 2. Early .uy and university network context

Uruguay's e-government agency reports that the .uy country-code domain was delegated in 1990 and that the first two registered sites were seciu.edu.uy and inco.edu.uy. SeCIU administered the domain through the Red Académica Uruguaya (RAU). These are institutional anchors and possible pathways to early student/staff homepages, not personal-page candidates themselves.

Source: https://www.gub.uy/agencia-gobierno-electronico-sociedad-informacion-conocimiento/comunicacion/noticias/uy-pasaron-20-anos-primer-dominio-web-uruguayo

### 3. Manya Page — early user-created collective site (1995; not a personal homepage)

A 2011 retrospective interview says the Peñarol fan site Manya Page began on 1 October 1995 and was among Uruguay's early websites. It also says founders César Luongo and Federico Peinado were university professors who initially hosted a version from the United States before Uruguay had full internet connectivity, and gathered contributions from fans abroad. This is not a personal homepage and the initial hosting location complicates national attribution, but it is a useful lead into early Uruguayan user-created web communities and personal contributor pages.

Source: https://www.padreydecano.com/cms/entrevistas/hace-21-anos-se-creaba-el-primer-sitio-web-de-penarol/

### 4. Montevideo Portal — early portal, not personal expression (November 1995)

A 2020 retrospective says Montevideo Portal's first version went online in November 1995 and included chat and a free Uruguayan email service. It is a portal rather than a personal page, but its early user base and email service may have generated personal pages, directories, and links.

Source: https://www.montevideo.com.uy/Archivo/25-anos-del-Portal-cuando-los-muchachos-de-Montevideo-se-hicieron-noticia-uc772696

## Research interpretation

- Do not treat .uy registration dates or the first institutional websites as evidence of personal pages.
- Chasque's reported open user-page hosting from August 1995 is currently the strongest route to actual individual sites. Find its domain/URL conventions, archived homepages, and old user directories.
- Search for terms such as “página personal”, “mi página”, “home page”, “páginas personales”, “usuarios de Chasque”, “Red Académica Uruguaya”, and “hospedaje” alongside local domain names.
- Search local newspapers, university newsletters, computing magazines, fan communities, and Internet Archive captures. Include pages hosted abroad only when the author's residence/community connection to Uruguay is evidenced.
- Keep collective fan sites, commercial portals, and institutional sites as context unless a specific personal page or author contribution can be isolated.

## Broadened chronology — email, networked communities, and Internet access (16 November 2026)

The project now records non-Web activity as well as websites. Uruguay has a much earlier and richer networked-communication chronology than the personal-homepage trail alone suggests.

### Early networked communication and online community

- **1986 — Chasque modem and external BBS/mailbox route (precursor / connected-service lead).** Chasque founder Roberto Bissio recalls installing a 1200-bps modem at the Instituto del Tercer Mundo, dialling Buenos Aires to reach Delphi (a BBS with X.25 access), and from there reaching GeoNet in London, where Chasque had an email mailbox. This is important prehistory for Uruguay's network culture, but the account does not establish a direct Internet connection in Uruguay in 1986. Classify as a dial-up BBS / intermediary online-service route, not as a verified domestic Internet node. Source: https://interred.wordpress.com/1986/02/26/chasque-nacio-solo-para-que-la-gente-se-comunique-mejor/ (first-person retrospective by Bissio; grade C).
- **1988-12-02 — INCO/Incouy stable international email.** A 2015 interview-based history in *El Observador* dates the first stable email connection from the Instituto de Computación (INCO), Universidad de la República, to 2 December 1988. Messages travelled by scheduled dial-up via Argentina and onward relays; the same account explicitly says Internet protocols were not yet established and traffic was manually forwarded. It is therefore a strong early international email milestone, not proof of an always-on TCP/IP Internet connection. A 2026 secondary account describes the same node as experimental UUCP; keep that protocol label provisional until the original INCO letter or technical record is inspected. Sources: https://www.elobservador.com.uy/nota/la-prehistoria-como-internet-llego-a-uruguay-20151254500/amp (interviews and reproduced email; grade B/C); https://btw.media/en/uruguay-uy-domain-email-before-internet-link-1988-1994 (secondary synthesis; grade C).
- **1989 — Chasque local BBS and discussion forums.** Bissio recalls that Chasque began answering local calls 24 hours a day and opened discussion forums for members; members could exchange email with one another. External communication was initially manual. The service was operated by ITeM in Montevideo and supported civil-society organizations. This is an early community online service; the local dial-up forum itself should be distinguished from Internet access. Source: https://interred.wordpress.com/1986/02/26/chasque-nacio-solo-para-que-la-gente-se-comunique-mejor/ (first-person retrospective; grade C).
- **1989–1991 — X.25 / APC inter-network email and UUCP.** Bissio describes Chasque's dedicated URUPAC data line and links to APC mailboxes at Alternex (Brazil), IGC (US), and GreenNet (UK); automatic external mail exchange via SeCIU began in 1990, and compressed UUCP mail packets over long-distance calls to the US in October 1991. These are important cross-border networked-communication milestones, but X.25/UUCP mail relaying should not be conflated with native TCP/IP connectivity. Source: same Bissio retrospective (grade C).
- **1992-09-08 — contemporary status report rules out direct Internet connectivity at that date.** Miguel Peirano's dated report to the Overseas Networkers list says Uruguay had “no direct Internet connection”; most international connections were UUCP over voice lines or X.25. It describes SeCIU's university email service, its polling of UUNET and an Argentine node several times daily, and Chasque as an APC civil-society node with about 120 users, connecting to IGC four times daily and SeCIU every two hours. It also says there was no longer a Fido node receiving outside Fido traffic, while Chasque was working with Fido enthusiasts on an rfmail channel. This is a particularly valuable contemporary checkpoint. Source: https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=UY&id=890202529785:497424205 (contemporary message reproduced by NSRC; grade A/B).
- **1993-08 to 1993-11 — remote Telnet/Gopher access through X.25.** Bissio dates Chasque's first real-time access to remote computers (Telnet) and document collections (Gopher) through an X.25 connection to Brazil's Alternex to August 1993. A November 1993 agreement with ANTEL and ITeM then let URUPAC users access Chasque email, Gopher and Telnet services. These are early Internet-service access milestones via an intermediary, not proof of a domestic always-on IP link. Source: Bissio retrospective (grade C).
- **1994 — academic direct Internet link; 1995 — public dial-up/Web access.** Bissio dates SECIU's Internet connection via ANTEL to March 1994, while noting restrictions on third-party access and a later interruption to the SECIU–Chasque line. Chasque says it began graphical Internet access via SLIP/PPP in August 1995, allowing members to browse the Web and publish their own pages. *El Observador* independently describes Internet becoming publicly visible in 1994 and Adinet dial-up following in 1995. Sources: Bissio retrospective (grade C); https://www.elobservador.com.uy/nota/la-prehistoria-como-internet-llego-a-uruguay-20151254500/amp (interview-based retrospective; grade B/C).

## Broadened chronology — primary INCO/Incouy UUCP and email evidence (16 November 2026)

### 2 December 1988 — experimental UUCP node operating regularly

The 2014 Universidad de la República / Facultad de Ingeniería volume *Aportes para la historia del Instituto de Computación* reproduces the original letter from Juan José Cabezas, Director of INCO, to the Faculty Dean, dated **8 December 1988**. It states that the experimental Uruguayan UUCP network node had been installed and was functioning regularly from **2 December 1988** on the fifth floor of the Faculty of Engineering. The letter says it was maintained by INCO's Programming Department and established with assistance from staff at the University of Buenos Aires. This is unusually strong documentary evidence for the local node and its start date (the surviving publication is a 2014 reproduction of the original letter).

The same volume reproduces early message headers and routing paths. It identifies a message from Gastón Gonnet at the University of Waterloo dated 1 December 1988, addressed through the route atina!dcfcen!InCo!jcabezas, and a message dated 2 December 1988 from INRIA routed via uunet!mcvax!sabre-madonna.inria.fr!steffen to the Uruguayan node. The book's narrative says the first non-Río de la Plata email received was Gonnet's message; the headers establish the message's send date and route, while the installation letter establishes the local node's operational date. Preserve this nuance rather than describing a single message as the first connection event.

- Source: https://www.fing.edu.uy/sites/default/files/Aportes_para_la_Historia_del_INCO_0.pdf (pp. 45–48; reproduced letter and mail headers).
- Evidence grade: **A/B** (reproduced contemporaneous administrative letter and original message headers; published in a 2014 institutional history).
- Category: UUCP node / international email / academic inter-network communication; operator: academic; network status: inter-network (UUCP), not native TCP/IP Internet.
- Additional detail: the volume says the node was the first Uruguayan node operating regularly and the only one until 1990; it later had more than 100 users from the Faculty of Engineering, the University of the Republic and other institutions. Treat the “first/only” characterization as retrospective testimony by the node's director, supported by the reproduced letter for the local node's operation.

### 1991-era UUCP registry record preserved in the same history

The volume reproduces an international UUCP map entry for InCouv / InCouy, identifying the operator as the University of Uruguay, Faculty of Engineering, INCO, naming Fernando Carpani and Juan Cabezas, and locating the node in Montevideo. The entry includes a #W update timestamp of 14 June 1989. This is useful corroboration of the node's identity and international registration, although the displayed historical alias/domain strings vary (InCouv, InCouy, InCouv.edu.ar).

The same volume quotes a May 1998 email saying that the Faculty's Gopher contained a list of Uruguayan academic institutions and preserved a 5 November 1991 UUCP registry entry for the Centro de Matemáticas (cmat) in Montevideo. This establishes a surviving Gopher-based institutional directory by the 1998 message, and a dated underlying UUCP registry entry from 1991; it does **not** establish that the Gopher service itself was online in 1991.

- Source: https://www.fing.edu.uy/sites/default/files/Aportes_para_la_Historia_del_INCO_0.pdf (pp. 46 and 52–53 in the extracted volume).

### Interpretation

Uruguay's chronology now has a stronger primary documentary base for the 1988 academic UUCP/email node. Keep it distinct from Chasque's 1989 local dial-up forums, 1990 automatic external email exchange, 1993 remote Telnet/Gopher access via X.25/Alternex, and 1994 direct SECIU Internet link. The INCO volume also provides names and host conventions for further discovery, but the listed user accounts are not public personal publishing evidence. Next: seek contemporaneous UUCP maps, SeCIU/RAU Gopher records, early mailing-list or Usenet archives, and Chasque service documentation.

## Contemporary 1996 evidence — Chasque Internet access and UUCP-connected BBS transition

A 23 April 1996 message from Gabriel Sere of Chasque to the Network Startup Resource Center describes the three main Uruguayan services and their network topology. It says Chasque (the Instituto del Tercer Mundo / APC node) had been online since 1989 and provided users interactive and PPP/SLIP access to all Internet services. It gives Chasque's Gopher and WWW addresses (gopher.item.org.uy and www.chasque.apc.org) and states that Chasque connected to ADINET over a 64 Kbit/s link, with a planned upgrade. The same message says that some BBSs were already doing UUCP with Chasque and were preparing to obtain leased lines to ADINET.

- Source: https://nsrc.org/db/lookup/report.php?fromISO=UY&id=890202529316:497425062
- Evidence grade: **A/B** (contemporary provider/operator message).
- Category: ISP / public Internet access / PPP-SLIP; Gopher and Web endpoints; BBS-to-UUCP interconnection.
- Interpretation: the record confirms that local BBS systems were participating in UUCP exchanges with Chasque and preparing a transition to leased-line Internet access. It does not identify the BBS names, prove that every BBS was already connected to the Internet, or date the start of the UUCP exchanges. Treat those BBSs as inter-network leads, not as automatically verified Internet nodes.

This is a valuable next discovery path: identify the BBS operators and their UUCP feeds from Chasque's user community, then search for surviving message areas, echo-mail groups, FidoNet/Usenet gateways and local operator recollections. ## Contemporary UUCP map and FidoNet/Internet gateway evidence — 1993

A UUCP map for Uruguay (u.ury.1) was posted to the Usenet group **comp.mail.maps** on 14 January 1997, but its embedded map identifies itself as the 24 August 1993 edition. It inventories SeCIU's central UUCP hub and a large set of connected institutional, nonprofit, commercial and academic nodes. The map is especially valuable because it exposes named operators, node purposes, routing relationships and update dates rather than only listing domain names.

### March 1993 — fidour, a FidoNet–Internet gateway exchanging with Chasque

The map identifies **fidour** as a “FidoNet-Internet Gateway,” operated by **Juan Carlos Alonso** in Montevideo. It records a 386DX/MS-DOS system running **UFGATE, FrontDoor and IMAIL**, with FidoNet address **4:850/0**. Its upstream route is igc!chasque, and the UUCP map explicitly lists fidour chasque(DAILY). The entry's operator update is dated **23 March 1993**.

This is direct evidence of a named Uruguay-based FidoNet gateway with a daily UUCP connection to Chasque by March 1993. The technical meaning needs care: the UFGATE 2.3 HOWTO (9 Dec 1988) describes UFGATE as a bridge for FidoNet↔UUCP mail and includes Netnews processing; it explicitly distinguishes UUCP and FidoNet from the TCP/IP Internet, saying they can be interconnected for mail without themselves being part of that Internet. The map's “FidoNet-Internet Gateway” label therefore records the operator/map's service description and Internet-mail capability, but does not prove that fidour itself ran TCP/IP or that Usenet articles actually traversed it. The same 1993 map identifies Chasque as Uruguay's APC node, with an X.25 address and scheduled UUCP links to CDP and SeCIU. This is stronger than the 1992 proposed rfmail channel, but the exact launch date, traffic content and direction of individual messages remain unknown.

- Source: https://groups.google.com/g/comp.mail.maps/c/NaiHPDCAKWg (1993 map embedded in a 14 Jan 1997 Usenet-posted shell archive; fidour entry around lines 353–371).
- UFGATE technical documentation: https://www.lns.com/papers/ufgate/UFGATE.HOW (version 2.3 dated 9 Dec 1988; sections I–II describe mail between FidoNet and UUCP/Internet, and glossary at lines 244–323 distinguishes TCP/IP Internet from UUCP/FidoNet and defines UFGATE's UUCP mail and Netnews functions). Tim Pozar's project history says UFGATE moved mail and conferences/news between FidoNet and UUCP/Usenet: https://www.lns.com/papers/ufgate.
- Evidence grade: **A/B** (contemporaneous technical map content reproduced in a dated Usenet archive; map version explicitly dated August 1993 and fidour operator update dated March 1993).
- Category: FidoNet/UUCP gateway; operator: individual; network status: FidoNet↔UUCP gateway; Internet-addressed email capability through upstream network; no direct TCP/IP claim.
- Country connection: operator and gateway node located in Montevideo, Uruguay.

### The broader 1993 SeCIU UUCP topology

The same map's SeCIU hub entry lists daily or scheduled links to dozens of nodes, including university departments, research institutes, government offices, NGOs, commercial organizations and the Chasque APC node. Examples include:

- **chasque** — Instituto del Tercer Mundo / APC node; daily exchange with SeCIU and four times daily with cdp; X.25 identifier recorded.
- **pangea** — “Red Telemática Intercultural,” contact Julia Pieruzzi Tirelli; scheduled SeCIU exchange.
- **plusul** — Fundación Plus Ultra; scheduled SeCIU exchange.
- **ciaru** — Centro de Investigación de Arte Rupestre del Uruguay; contact Mario Consens.
- **urunet** — Comax S.A. (URUNET), a commercial node; contact Luis Sibils Bove.
- **varela** — Colegio Nacional José Pedro Varela; contact Alejandro Ferreira.

The map's embedded August 1993 version includes node records with update dates reaching back to 1991–1992. Those dates establish the reported node/map-entry updates, not necessarily each system's first operational day. The map is a discovery index for further examination of local mailing lists, FidoNet echo areas, UUCP news feeds and institutional archives. It should not be treated as proof that every listed node hosted a public Internet service or website.

### Interpretation

Uruguay's network chronology now includes a concrete FidoNet-to-Internet gateway by March 1993, in addition to the earlier INCO UUCP node, Chasque's APC/X.25 links, and the later RAU WWW and Chasque PPP/SLIP services. The gateway record gives a named operator and a specific daily route through Chasque, so it should be retained as its own milestone rather than folded into the general “BBS precursor” category.


### Independent corroboration — FidoNet's approved Internet gateway list (July 1994)

A second, independent route confirms fidour's gateway role. A FidoNet gateway registry, reproduced in a Serbian FidoNet conference archive in a message dated **5 September 1995**, carries a source-list revision date of **6 July 1994** and includes **fidour 4:850/0** among the sites approved by the FidoNet hostmaster to gate mail from FidoNet to the Internet. The accompanying text explains that listed gateways had passed hostmaster tests and were authorized to carry the GUUCP flag. This corroborates that the Montevideo node was not merely labelled as a gateway in a local UUCP map: it was also present in the wider FidoNet gateway approval list by the July 1994 revision.

- Source: https://oldsezam.net/Conference/KOMUNIK.8/email?p=18 (message email.1824, dated 5 Sep 1995; embedded gateway list revision 6 Jul 1994).
- Evidence grade: **B** (contemporary FidoNet conference repost of an automated hostmaster registry; underlying list is explicitly revision-dated).
- Qualification: the source confirms approval/listing by July 1994, not the gateway's exact launch date or whether it accepted both directions of mail. The 1993 UUCP map establishes the Chasque daily route; the 1994 registry independently establishes its recognized FidoNet-to-Internet gateway status.


## Earlier FidoNet presence — contemporary FidoNews account (9 November 1992)

A FidoNews issue dated **9 November 1992** contains a contemporaneous account of Uruguay's early FidoNet participation. Contributor Pablo Kleinman writes that he and Hector Gomez (an Uruguayan sysop living in Argentina) had worked to bring Uruguay into FidoNet; Gomez's cousin **Juan Camps**, in Montevideo, installed the first Uruguayan node with sponsorship from a large local computer distributor. Camps later went offline, and the small Uruguayan region was removed from the FidoNet nodelist after prolonged failure to communicate. The same account says that **Juan Carlos Alonso** was then receiving mail from Randy Bush and was being helped to reconnect with the wider Zone 4 network.

A second contributor in the same issue describes the former Uruguayan region as consisting of one private system and two sysops, and says its nodelist entry was removed after the node stopped answering. This provides independent contemporary context for a small, fragile FidoNet presence before the better-documented March 1993 `fidour` gateway record.

- Source: https://groups.google.com/g/comp.org.fidonet/c/WqdCabsEdPA (FidoNews vol. 9 no. 45, 9 Nov 1992; discussion at pp. 3–7 of the reproduced newsletter, especially lines 178–199 and 306–312 / 400–407).
- Evidence grade: **B** (contemporary FidoNet community newsletter; participant testimony, not a surviving node log or nodelist).
- Category: FidoNet node/community; operator: individual/community; status: **precursor context**, not yet a verified Internet-connected node. The reference to Alonso receiving mail from Randy Bush is a promising interconnection lead, but the route and protocol are not specified in this account. Do not assume that the earlier Juan Camps node was Internet-connected.
- Follow-up: seek FidoNet nodelists for Region 85 / Uruguay, Juan Camps' node name and sponsor, and messages between Alonso and Randy Bush; compare with the March 1993 `fidour` map and July 1994 hostmaster gateway registry.


## Primary FidoNet nodelists — Uruguay Region 85 (1991–1992)

The preserved weekly FidoNet nodelists make the 1992 newsletter's brief account much more concrete. The 1991 archive's **24 May** nodelist does not contain Region 85, while the **7 June 1991** nodelist already lists:

- **Region 85 — Red Banda Oriental**, Uruguay; regional coordinator **Juan Camps**, Montevideo phone contact.
- **Host 850 — FidoNet Uruguaya**, Montevideo, also operated by Juan Camps.
- **Node 4:850/1 — Datamatic**, Ciudad Vieja, Montevideo, Juan Camps.

The **13 September 1991** nodelist still lists Camps and Datamatic. By **27 December 1991**, the same host has three nodes: Camps / Datamatic (4:850/1), **Andrés Borthagaray / Superandres (4:850/2)**, and **Juan Carlos Alonso / Iosys (4:850/3)**. This identifies Alonso's actual FidoNet system and demonstrates that the Uruguayan region expanded beyond the single initial node during 1991.

The **13 November 1992** nodelist no longer contains Region 85, consistent with the 9 November 1992 FidoNews participant account that the region had been removed after its small number of nodes went offline / stopped communicating. The nodelists therefore establish an operational Uruguay FidoNet region no later than 7 June 1991, its three named systems by 27 December 1991, and its absence from the global list by 13 November 1992. ### Alonso's mail from Randy Bush — a possible inter-zone route, not yet a proven message path

The 9 November 1992 FidoNews discussion says Juan Carlos Alonso was receiving mail from Randy Bush. The 18 April 1994 FidoNews later includes a direct explanation by Randy Bush: mail for Zone 4 had been held at his FidoNet node **1:105/42** for **4:4/1** since about 1991, after he stopped delivering mail to Argentina. This provides a plausible FidoNet inter-zone mechanism that could explain cross-zone mail reaching Uruguay, and makes the Alonso clue more specific. However, the 1994 explanation is about Zone 4 routing generally and does not explicitly identify Alonso or his 1992 message. No header or routing log yet proves that Alonso's mail traversed 1:105/42 → 4:4/1.

- FidoNews 11-16, 18 Apr 1994, “Routing Z1 to Z4”: https://groups.google.com/g/comp.org.fidonet/c/qMKZzzq8I6Y (Randy Bush's explanation at pp. 13–14 / lines 657–679).
- Evidence grade: **B** (participant's contemporary explanation of Zone 1–Zone 4 FidoNet mail holding/routing).
- Interpretation: keep this as a route hypothesis until the original message headers, a Zone 4 gateway log, or a contemporaneous nodelist entry for 4:4/1 confirms the exact chain.

### Revised milestone

**7 June 1991** is now the earliest located contemporary global FidoNet nodelist listing for Uruguay in the preserved weekly archive; the 24 May 1991 issue is a negative checkpoint. This is a dated FidoNet network milestone, not a date for Uruguay's first Internet connection, first email, or first personal website.

## Earlier Chasque communications — circa 1986–1991

Roberto Bissio's first-person history of Chasque provides a finer-grained chronology than the later shorthand that dates Chasque to 1989. He recalls that **around 1986** (explicitly: the exact date is impossible to know), ITeM installed a 1,200-bps modem on an Apple II. It dialled Delphi, a BBS in Buenos Aires with X.25 access, and through it reached GeoNet in London, where ITeM maintained a collective email mailbox. The mailbox was named **chasque** and was used to communicate with the Third World Network secretariat in Malaysia and international correspondents; staff also retrieved information from remote databases. This is a Uruguay-based organization's early email and remote-information activity, but the mailbox and GeoNet host were outside Uruguay and the route was via commercial BBS/X.25 services—not a domestic Internet host or a direct TCP/IP connection.

Bissio then dates the local Chasque system to **1989**: an XT computer and four-modem card enabled 24-hour local dial-in and discussion forums. Users could exchange mail with one another, while external communication was initially handled manually. After ANTEL introduced packet data, Chasque established a dedicated URUPAC line and exchanged messages with accounts at Chasque in Alternex (Brazil), IGC (United States) and GreenNet (United Kingdom). In **1990**, Chasque automated external email exchange through SECIU using Waffle; each night the system exchanged message bundles with the university computer. In **October 1991**, it began sending compressed email packets via UUCP over long-distance calls to the United States. This sequence distinguishes an offshore collective mailbox (c.1986), a local BBS (1989), automated external email (1990), and UUCP/APC network interconnection (1991).

- Primary participant account: https://interred.wordpress.com/1986/02/26/chasque-nacio-solo-para-que-la-gente-se-comunique-mejor/ (Roberto Bissio; account text at lines 7–17).
- Independent retrospective corroboration: https://rimisp.org/wp-content/files_mf/1359136070LibroInternetii.pdf, *Impactos sociales de las Tecnologías de Información y Comunicación (TIC) en Latinoamérica y el Caribe* (FLACSO/IDRC, 2001), chapter on local impact in Montevideo; it places ITeM's use of ICT in 1986 and Chasque's first civic-use provider role in 1989.
- Evidence grade: **C** for the exact early dates (first-person retrospective account; 2001 research volume based on interviews with Bissio and other network operators). The account is detailed but still retrospective; no original 1986 mail headers or 1989 system logs have yet been located.
- Category: email / BBS / store-and-forward gateway / UUCP; operator: nonprofit/community; network status changes by phase—remote email via foreign BBS/X.25, local dial-up BBS, then external packet/UUCP email. Do not label the 1986 mailbox as a Uruguay-based Internet node.
